Where the hardware is. Every example exports ArduPilot missions and a launcher that checks, flies and draws them. All four have flown in ArduPilot's simulator (SITL): the real autopilot firmware on a simulated airframe. Next comes one real aircraft.

Any of the four can be flown live in ArduPilot's simulator; the launcher then draws the flight against its plan as an animated picture. The choreography show takes about two minutes of flight. What the simulator proves — SITL runs ArduPilot's own firmware, so the parameter names, mission upload, arming and flight timing are the real autopilot's. Flying it found and fixed real defects: renamed parameters, the mission upload handshake, and a timing model that assumed constant speed. — It does not prove the airframe, wind, GPS error or radio loss. No mission has flown a real aircraft yet; one real aircraft is the next step. Detail — The air bridge is the clearest of the four. The corridor is the planned entity and drones are units of flow on it, so the plan is about where the pipe narrows rather than where each aircraft goes. The classical planner remains the oracle the quantum one has to beat. — Its end customers are the aerial-services contractors who fly for fire and rescue. Common questions — "What about payload?" A camera sets the overlap the coverage pattern must hold; a spray tank trades mass against endurance and forces refill stops into the schedule. The allocator already models capacity limits and per-task takeoff and landing overheads, so the plan works around whatever the operator already owns. Scope — Today the example runs four drones on sixteen qubits on a laptop, with the quantum solver executing every transition. What the company claims is the API: it will not need rewriting when larger machines arrive.
