The first two rows are the business; the rest are why those two rows grow. The arithmetic — Drone Industry Insights' Market Map 2026 lists 1,413 drone companies worldwide, 177 of them software companies and 295 platform makers. Roughly 500 of the 1,413 are European: Germany 100, the UK 78, France 59 and Switzerland 56 are counted, the rest inferred. — That gives about 50 to 100 European software and ground-control vendors and 100 to 200 airframe makers and integrators: 150 to 300 organisations. The slide uses 150, the low end, since not every one needs multi-aircraft planning. — 150 at a £40k licence is £6m a year. The nine licences in year three are 6% of it. The count becomes a named list when the vendors are screened, which is on the ninety-day plan. Common questions — "Which buyer feels it first?" The ground-control and fleet-software vendor. Its operator customers are moving from one aircraft per pilot to several, and the workflow that breaks is re-planning a multi-aircraft mission when a unit drops out or a task is added. The vendor either staffs an optimisation team or loses the fleet deal to a product that has the feature. — "Why not sell straight to operators?" About a dozen hold corridor authorisations in the UK, they buy one at a time, and a vendor reaches all of them at once. — "Why drones and not pharma?" A fleet can be demonstrated in a week; a drug programme cannot. Detail — Two adjacent markets were cut from the table for readability: drone light shows, which want collision-aware formation transitions exported to flight controllers, and the antibiotic-precursor work, where VQE ranks alternative synthesis routes — regulated, partner-led, later, and carrying no budget in this plan. — Civil applications come first: defence and dual-use enquiries go through an export-control review before any engagement.
