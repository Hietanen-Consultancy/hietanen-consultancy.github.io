The commercial answer to the two objections every technical buyer raises: we have no training data, and we cannot tell whether a retrained model got better. Both are true of chat, and neither is true of a typed decision. Detail — Outcomes: few companies own a training corpus, and building one is a project nobody funds. Every company owns outcomes: which team took the case, whether the claim was approved, whether the transaction turned out to be fraud. Those labels are produced by running the business. — Measurement: nobody can say whether a fine-tuned chat model beats the one it replaced. A typed decision is scored with accuracy, log loss, Brier score and calibration error on held-out cases, so a model that reports 90% confidence can be checked to be right about 90% of the time. — Return: above the confidence threshold, the software acts; below it, the case goes to a person. The share handled without a human is measured rather than asserted, which is also what lets a regulated buyer approve it. — When the output really is prose, the judge is typed too: old against new as one closed choice, asked both ways round. Common questions — "How would you know the retrain helped?" Public benchmarks usually fall after a domain fine-tune, so a team watching MMLU concludes the model went backwards exactly when it got better at its own job. The typed arena compares the two models on the customer's own work instead. — "What accuracy do customers get?" It is measured per customer, inside the customer's environment, on their own held-out cases. The figure leaves only with their consent, and the data never leaves at all.
